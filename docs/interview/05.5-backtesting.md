# Module 05.5 — Backtesting

Covers roadmap step 5.5: replaying `evaluate()` (step 4's pure engine) over historical `Candle`
data instead of a single live "now," and the honest limits of scoring a result when the domain
model underneath has no concept of a trade yet. The theme running through this module is the same
one module 04 ended on — what a schema *doesn't* model constrains what a consumer built on top of
it can honestly claim — applied to a new consumer instead of a new field.

---

## A. Same `evaluate()`, two different callers

### A1. `apps/backtesting/tasks.py::run_backtest` imports `HISTORY_CANDLE_COUNT` from `apps/strategy/tasks.py` instead of defining its own constant. Why does that matter beyond avoiding two lines of duplication?

If `run_backtest` picked its own warm-up window size, a backtest's replay of history could produce
a *different* trigger decision than `evaluate_strategy` would have made live at that exact point
in time — not because the strategy behaves differently, but because the two code paths handed
`evaluate()` different amounts of context. Importing the same constant guarantees a backtest is
asking the exact question live evaluation asks ("given the last 200 candles ending here, does
this trigger?"), just asked many times over history instead of once for "now." A backtest that
quietly used a different window wouldn't be simulating live behaviour at all — it'd be simulating
a different, unintended strategy.

> **In this repo:** `from cryptovira.apps.strategy.tasks import HISTORY_CANDLE_COUNT` — a direct
> cross-app import, not a copied literal `200`. If Step 4's constant changes, this app changes
> with it automatically rather than silently drifting out of sync.

### A2. `run_backtest` builds `window = series[max(0, i - HISTORY_CANDLE_COUNT + 1) : i + 1]` inside a loop over every in-range candle, rather than computing indicators once over the whole date range. Why a rolling window per candle instead of one vectorized pass?

Because indicator warm-up is stateful in a way that matters here: `evaluate()` needs to see
*exactly* the candles that would have been available at that point in history — no more (that
would leak future data into a past decision) and no fewer (that would starve the indicator of
context it should have had). A single `talib.SMA(all_closes, timeperiod=200)` call computed once
over the *entire* range technically produces the right answer for the same mathematical reason a
rolling window does — TA-Lib's own sliding-window algorithms are already correct — but expressing
it as one call over the full array obscures the actual question being asked at each candle. Doing
it as an explicit per-candle window keeps `run_backtest` provably asking `evaluate_strategy`'s
exact question at every step, at the cost of recomputing overlapping work — an explicit,
legible trade-off, not a performance-blind one: still O(n · window), the same complexity class
TA-Lib's own vectorized functions run at internally.

**Drill:** for a backtest over a year of 1-minute candles (~525,600 in-range candles), estimate
the number of `evaluate()` calls this makes, and roughly how that scales if a `StrategyConfig` has
three conditions instead of one. Is per-candle re-evaluation the right trade-off at that scale, or
does the vectorized alternative (compute each indicator once over the whole array, then index into
it per candle) become worth the loss of "obviously identical to live evaluation" this module just
argued for?

## B. A forward-return proxy — and being honest about what it isn't

### B1. The old system's backtest computed a real `total_r_r` per triggered position — stop-loss, take-profit, risk/reward. This step's `total_forward_return` is a much simpler close-to-close percentage move `horizon_candles` after a trigger. What concretely is missing from `StrategyConfig` that makes the old computation impossible to reproduce here?

A notion of a *trade* at all. The old `total_r_r` needed a `side` (BUY/SELL — which direction is
the trigger a signal for?), a stop-loss threshold, and fibonacci take-profit levels — none of
which exist anywhere in `apps/strategy/schema.py`'s `Condition`/`StrategyConfig` (ADR 0008).
`StrategyConfig` is a flat AND-chain of `indicator`/`operator`/`value` conditions: `evaluate()`
answers "is this logical expression true right now," full stop. There's no field to read a side
from, no stop distance, nothing to simulate a position against. Reproducing the old computation
would mean designing and adding those fields first — which is real, undecided product work (how
should stop-loss be expressed? per-condition or per-strategy? percentage or ATR-based?), not
something to guess silently inside a backtest task.

> **In this repo:** [ADR 0010](../adr/0010-backtest-forward-return-proxy.md)'s Context section
> states this gap explicitly and names the specific old fields (`side`, `stop_loss_threshold`,
> fibo levels, a bound `BinanceProfile`) that have no analogue here.

### B2. Given that gap, what does `win_rate`/`total_forward_return` actually measure, and — precisely — what can't you conclude from a strategy scoring, say, a 65% `win_rate`?

It measures: "after this condition became true, did the close price move up over the next
`horizon_candles` candles, and by how much, on average." That's genuinely useful for iterating on
a *condition* — does "RSI crosses above 70" tend to precede upward moves in this data or not. What
you can't conclude: whether a real trader following this strategy would have made money. A 65%
`win_rate` says nothing about position sizing, what a stop-loss would have done intraday between
candles, transaction costs/slippage, or — critically — whether the strategy author even *intended*
a long position on this trigger. A condition like "RSI crosses below 30" (classically a
mean-reversion long signal) and "RSI crosses above 70" (classically a momentum long *or*
reversion-short signal, depending on the trader) are scored by the exact same rule here, because
the proxy has no way to know which direction the author meant.

> **In this repo:** `apps/backtesting/models.py`'s module docstring and ADR 0010's Decision
> section both call this out directly rather than let a `win_rate` field imply more precision than
> it has.

### B3. Why doesn't the proxy at least ask the strategy author to specify a side (long/short) as part of creating a `Backtest`, even without touching `StrategyConfig` itself — a `Backtest.side` field, say?

It's a real option that was considered and set aside (not because it's wrong, but because it's a
bigger step than this one needs to take first) — see ADR 0010's Decision section. Adding a `side`
field only to `Backtest` (not `StrategyConfig`) would let backtest-time scoring assume a direction
without touching Step 4's schema at all — a smaller change than extending `StrategyConfig`. But it
was deliberately not built here: a side chosen at backtest time, disconnected from the condition
itself, is *also* a guess (nothing forces it to match what the condition's author actually
intended), and Step 6's real order/position model is coming regardless — building a half-measure
now (a `side` field with no execution to back it, still no stop-loss/take-profit) risks becoming
schema debt once the real thing exists. The side-agnostic proxy is honest about being provisional
in a way a bolt-on `side` field pretending to be a real backtest wouldn't be.

**Drill:** ADR 0010's "We would revisit if" section says Step 6 should add a *second* backtest
mode simulating real positions, rather than replacing this proxy outright. Argue the other side:
why might keeping two backtest result shapes (a fast proxy and a real position-simulation mode)
long-term be worse than picking one and migrating every `Backtest` row to it? What would the
migration path look like if the proxy were deprecated instead of kept?

## C. Don't fabricate a result from data that doesn't exist — twice over

### C1. `Backtest.scored_trigger_count` and `Backtest.trigger_count` are tracked as two separate fields rather than one. Walk through a concrete case where they differ, and why the difference is reported rather than silently resolved one way or the other.

A trigger at a candle less than `horizon_candles` before `end_time` has no real forward candle to
measure a return against — `run_backtest` checks `forward_index < len(series)` before scoring, and
skips accumulating into `total_forward_return`/`win_count` when it's false, while still
incrementing `trigger_count`. Concretely: a backtest with `horizon_candles=10` and a trigger on
the very last in-range candle needs 10 *real* future candles (drawn from `tail`, i.e. actual
ingested data past `end_time`) to score it — if fewer than 10 exist (or none), that trigger is
real (the condition genuinely fired) but unscoreable. Reporting `trigger_count=50,
scored_trigger_count=43` is more honest than either silently dropping those 7 triggers from the
count entirely (undercounting what actually happened) or scoring them with a synthetic/zero return
(fabricating a number from data that was never observed).

### C2. This is the second time this codebase has drawn this exact line — module 04, section D, covers the first. Name it, and say precisely what's structurally identical between the two.

`InsufficientDataError` (`apps/strategy/indicators.py`/`engine.py`): when an indicator's warm-up
period exceeds the candle history supplied, `evaluate()` doesn't guess a value — it raises, and
the caller (`evaluate_strategy`, and here, `run_backtest` too) records "not enough data" rather
than a fabricated `triggered` result. Both cases are the same shape: *insufficient real data at
one edge of a window* (the front, for indicator warm-up; the back, for forward-return horizon)
must be reported as "can't answer" rather than papered over with a default. The specific mechanism
differs (an exception there, a separate counter here) because the two situations differ slightly —
insufficient warm-up data means the *trigger decision itself* is unreliable, so nothing gets
recorded at all for that candle; insufficient forward data means the trigger decision is fine but
its *score* is unknowable, so the trigger still counts, just not toward the rate/return
aggregates.

> **In this repo:** `run_backtest`'s inline comment at the `else` branch of the `forward_index`
> check says this explicitly: "counted in trigger_count but deliberately excluded from
> scored_trigger_count/win_rate rather than fabricating a return from data that doesn't exist" —
> the same phrase this module uses, not a coincidence.

**Drill:** `run_backtest` still fetches `tail` (up to `horizon_candles` real candles past
`end_time`) specifically so triggers near the boundary *can* be scored when the data exists. What
would change about `scored_trigger_count`'s meaning if `tail` were removed entirely (i.e., only
`in_range` candles were ever available to score against)? Would every trigger in the last
`horizon_candles` candles of the range necessarily go unscored, or does it depend on where exactly
in the range they fall?

## D. Why this task doesn't need the idempotency machinery every prior step built

### D1. `ingest_candles`, `evaluate_strategy`, and `record_signal` (Steps 3-5) each have a `UniqueConstraint` plus a `bulk_create(ignore_conflicts=True)` or `OneToOneField` guarding against a redelivered task creating a duplicate row. `run_backtest` has none of that. Is this an oversight, or a deliberate difference — and what's the actual distinction?

Deliberate. Those three tasks each *create new rows representing a discrete event* (a candle
closing, an evaluation running, a signal firing) — redelivery under `task_acks_late` genuinely
risks a second, spurious event-row for work already recorded. `run_backtest` is different in kind:
it doesn't create a new `Backtest` row at all (one already exists, in `PENDING`, before the task
ever runs) — it *recomputes* that one row's result fields from scratch every time it runs, and a
redelivery recomputing the identical deterministic answer and overwriting the same fields with it
is not a duplicate side effect to prevent. It's the same category of operation as re-running a
report: safe, idempotent *by virtue of being a pure recomputation*, without needing a database
constraint to make it so.

> **In this repo:** [ADR 0010](../adr/0010-backtest-forward-return-proxy.md)'s Consequences
> section states this as a named, deliberate scope decision, not silence on the topic.

### D2. Concretely: what *would* go wrong if two `run_backtest.delay(same_id)` calls executed concurrently — is there truly nothing to worry about?

There's a narrower race than the ones Steps 3-5 guard against, and it's real: two concurrent runs
both read the same `Backtest` row, both compute (possibly using slightly different candle data if
new candles were ingested between the two reads — unlikely mid-backtest but not impossible), and
whichever `save()` call lands last silently overwrites the other's results. Nothing crashes and no
duplicate row appears — the final state is just "whichever run finished second," not
necessarily the more complete or correct one if the underlying candle data actually changed
between them. This is deliberately not solved here (no locking, no version check) — a `Backtest`
is admin-triggered, one row per admin action, so concurrent re-runs of the *same* row are an
unlikely operational scenario, not a routine one the way `evaluate_strategy` redelivery is under
normal `task_acks_late` operation.

**Drill:** if `run_backtest` were changed to accept concurrent runs as routine (say, a future API
lets a user re-run a backtest anytime, including while a previous run is still executing), what's
the smallest change that would make the race in D2 safe — a status check at the top of the task
refusing to run if `status == RUNNING` already? A `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE` claim step, the same
tool module 05's section D previewed for `NotificationDelivery`? Compare the two and say which
failure mode each one actually closes.

## E. A dedicated Celery queue — the third one added on top of Step 1's single `default`

### E1. `run_backtest` gets its own `backtesting` queue rather than reusing `strategy` (where `evaluate_strategy` already lives). What's the concrete operational risk of sharing, given both tasks touch the same domain?

Queue sharing means shared *ordering and capacity*, not just a shared topic. `evaluate_strategy`
is time-sensitive — it's beat-scheduled to run within minutes of each interval boundary, and a
late live evaluation is a missed or stale signal. `run_backtest` is comparatively heavy and
open-ended: a backtest over a year of 1-minute candles is hundreds of thousands of `evaluate()`
calls (see section A's drill), potentially minutes of wall-clock work on one worker. If both
shared a queue, a long-running backtest dequeued just before a scheduled `evaluate_strategy` tick
would delay that tick behind it under `worker_prefetch_multiplier=1` — the same correctness-over-
throughput setting (`celery.py`) that makes a slow task never "hoard" work also means it fully
occupies its worker slot until done. Separating the queues means a backtest's runtime has zero
effect on live evaluation's latency.

> **In this repo:** `celery.py`'s `task_routes` comment states this reasoning inline, next to
> the identical justification already given for why `orders` (Step 6) gets its own queue rather
> than sharing `market`.

**Drill:** `docker/entrypoint.sh`'s worker role listens on `default,market,strategy,orders,
notifications,backtesting` — one process, six queues, `--concurrency=4`. Does adding a *queue*
actually guarantee the isolation section E1 describes, or does that guarantee only hold once a
*separate worker process* is dedicated to consuming `backtesting` alone? What's true today, with
one shared worker process listening to all six queues at once, versus what Step 8's "production
hardening" (load/failure testing, real deployment topology) might change about this?

## F. Questions you should be able to ask back

1. `run_backtest` validates `end_time > start_time` and "no candles in range" explicitly, but lets
   any other exception propagate uncaught to Celery's redelivery machinery, same as
   `evaluate_strategy`. Given `run_backtest` isn't idempotent the way `evaluate_strategy` is
   (section D), what actually happens to a `Backtest` row's `status` if the task raises partway
   through the replay loop — does it stay stuck at `RUNNING` forever, and is that acceptable?
2. `Backtest.horizon_candles` defaults to 10, applied uniformly to every trigger in a given
   backtest. Would a strategy with a `crosses_above` (edge, fires once) condition and one with a
   `gt` (level, re-fires every tick it holds — see module 04, section C) want the *same* horizon
   for a fair comparison, or does the operator type change what "forward return after this
   trigger" should even mean?
3. `BacktestAdmin.readonly_fields` locks down every result field but leaves `strategy`,
   `start_time`, `end_time`, and `horizon_candles` editable even after a backtest has completed.
   What happens if an admin edits `end_time` on an already-`COMPLETED` row without re-running it —
   does anything in this codebase catch that the stored results no longer match the row's own
   config, or is that an honest gap?
4. This module and module 04 both end on the same idea from different angles — what a schema
   doesn't model, a consumer built on top of it can't honestly claim to answer. Step 6 will add a
   real order/position model. Once it exists, what's the actual migration path for *existing*
   `Backtest` rows computed under this proxy — do they get recomputed, relabeled, or left alone
   as a historical record of what the proxy said at the time?
